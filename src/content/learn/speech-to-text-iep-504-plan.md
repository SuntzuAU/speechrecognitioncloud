Most IEP meetings reach a moment where everyone agrees on the same thing: this student knows far more than their written work shows. The ideas are there when they talk. They fall apart on the page — because every word has to be spelled, or every letter has to be formed by a hand that won't cooperate.

Speech to text is one of the most direct answers to that problem, and it is one the law already expects teams to think about. This guide covers what the federal rules say, how teams usually write dictation into an IEP or a 504 plan, which students it helps, and the practical limits worth knowing before the meeting.

## Can speech to text be written into an IEP as assistive technology?

**Yes. Under the Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA), the IEP team must consider whether a child needs assistive technology every time it develops, reviews or revises the IEP — and speech-to-text software is a recognised example.** The requirement is in the IDEA regulations at [34 CFR 300.324](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/regs/b/d/300.324), and the U.S. Department of Education's 2024 guidance on assistive technology gives speech-to-text software for writing an essay as an example of a device a child may need.

The same guidance makes two points that matter for dictation in particular:

- **Consideration is for every child with an IEP**, not only students with a particular disability category.
- **School-provided assistive technology can be used at home** when the IEP team decides that home use is needed for the child to receive a free appropriate public education. For a writing tool, that usually means homework.

Whether dictation goes into a *specific* student's IEP is the team's call, based on that student's needs. The point is that it belongs on the table, and parents are entitled to raise it. The Department's [myths and facts guidance on assistive technology](https://sites.ed.gov/idea/idea-files/myths-and-facts-surrounding-assistive-technology-devices-and-services/) is worth bringing to the meeting.

## What is the difference between an IEP and a 504 plan for dictation?

**An IEP is written under IDEA for students who qualify for special education; a 504 plan is written under Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act for students with a disability who need accommodations but not necessarily special education. Dictation can sit in either.**

| | IEP | 504 plan |
|---|---|---|
| Law | Individuals with Disabilities Education Act (IDEA) | Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act of 1973 |
| Who it covers | Students who qualify for special education in one of IDEA's categories | Students with a disability that substantially limits a major life activity, including learning |
| What it provides | Specially designed instruction, related services, and assistive technology | Accommodations and modifications so the student can access learning |
| How dictation usually appears | As an assistive technology device, sometimes with training as an assistive technology service | As an accommodation for written assignments, homework or note-taking |

In practice, a student with dyslexia who is making progress in reading but struggles to get writing down may have a 504 plan with dictation as a written-work accommodation. A student with a significant writing disability may have an IEP with dictation listed as assistive technology alongside specialised writing instruction. Your school's team will know which route applies.

## Which students benefit most from speech to text?

**Speech to text helps most where the student's ideas are ahead of their spelling or handwriting — typically students with dyslexia, dysgraphia, or fine-motor and physical disabilities.**

- **Dyslexia.** The barrier is often spelling rather than ideas. Every uncertain word becomes a stop-and-think, so the student retreats to short, safe words they know how to spell. Dictation lets them use the vocabulary they'd use out loud. There's more on this in our guide to [speech to text for students with dyslexia](/learn/speech-recognition-students-dyslexia/).
- **Dysgraphia.** The barrier is the physical and motor act of writing — slow, effortful, sometimes painful, and hard to read afterwards. Dictation takes the hand out of composing. See [dictation for students with dysgraphia](/learn/speech-recognition-students-dysgraphia/).
- **Fine-motor and physical disabilities.** For students for whom typing is also slow or tiring, voice may be the fastest route to written output.
- **ADHD and working-memory difficulties.** Some students lose the end of a sentence while they are still writing the beginning. Saying it in one go can help them keep hold of the thought.

What dictation does **not** do is teach reading or spelling. It is an access tool, not a literacy intervention, and it works best alongside structured literacy instruction rather than instead of it.

Age matters too. Recognition accuracy for children's voices improves through early adolescence, so students from around middle school age generally get the most reliable results. Younger students can use it, but expect more corrections.

## What should an IEP or 504 plan say about dictation?

**Plans that work well are specific: which tasks, which settings, which equipment, who trains the student, and how dictated work gets checked.** The exact wording is a decision for the team and the district — this is what teams commonly cover:

1. **Which tasks.** Extended writing, essays, homework, note-taking, or all written work. Some students dictate a first draft and edit by keyboard.
2. **Which settings.** Classroom, resource room, home. If homework is included, the plan should say so, since the guidance allows school-provided assistive technology to be used at home when the team decides it's needed.
3. **Equipment.** A Windows computer and a headset microphone. A laptop's built-in microphone works but struggles in a noisy classroom, and every misrecognition is extra proofreading.
4. **Training.** Who will set it up and show the student how to use it. For this tool that is short — there is no voice training to sit through — but someone should own it.
5. **A quiet space where possible.** Dictating in the middle of a group activity is harder for everyone. Many schools use a corner of the room, a resource room or the library.
6. **Proofreading.** Dictated drafts have a different kind of error: the right-sounding wrong word — *their* for *there*, a misheard name. Build in a read-back step.

## How does a student actually use it?

**The student places the cursor where the text should go, presses a hotkey, speaks naturally, and the punctuated sentence appears.** It works at the cursor in any Windows application — Microsoft Word, Google Docs or Google Classroom in a browser, an online assignment form, a learning management system — because it works at the operating-system level rather than as a plugin for one app.

That matters for school because students rarely write in only one place. A tool that only works inside one word processor stops being useful the moment the assignment is in a browser form.

A few features are worth knowing about for students specifically:

- **Automatic punctuation.** Students don't have to say "comma" and "period" — punctuation is added as they speak, and they can [dictate punctuation out loud](/support/spoken-punctuation/) when they want control.
- **Custom vocabulary.** Add the names of characters in a set text, a teacher's name, or subject terms the student uses often.
- **No voice profile.** There is nothing to train and nothing to redo when the student changes computer.

## Can students use speech to text on state tests?

**It depends on the state and the test.** Speech-to-text is handled as a testing accommodation under each state's own assessment rules, and some tests permit it only for particular items, particular students, or through the test platform's own tools rather than third-party software.

Two practical points:

- **Check your state's current accessibility and accommodations manual** for the specific assessment. These manuals are updated regularly.
- **Keep classwork and testing aligned.** If dictation is how the student writes all year, the plan should address what happens on test day, so it isn't a surprise.

The same applies to college entrance exams, which have their own accommodations processes.

## What can't speech to text do, and does it work on Chromebooks?

Being straight about the limits, because these are the ones that come up in IEP meetings:

- **Windows only.** Speech Recognition Cloud runs on Windows 10 and Windows 11. It does **not** run on Chromebooks, iPads or Macs. Many US districts issue Chromebooks, so a student may need a Windows device, or the team may need a different tool. A companion mobile app for phones is available on paid plans.
- **It needs an internet connection.** Recognition happens in the cloud. Offline use isn't possible.
- **It doesn't write for the student.** It types what they say. Planning, structure and argument are still the student's work — which is exactly why it is an accommodation and not a shortcut.
- **It doesn't read text aloud.** Students who also need text-to-speech for reading will need a separate tool.
- **Accuracy depends on the environment.** A headset and a reasonably quiet space make a real difference.

## Is it worth trying before the meeting?

Three questions narrow it down:

1. **Does the student's written work say much less than they can say out loud?** If yes, dictation is worth testing.
2. **Do they have access to a Windows computer at school or home?** If not, it's the wrong tool for now.
3. **Will someone help them build the proofreading habit?** If yes, it has a good chance of sticking.

If all three are yes, install the free version, have the student dictate one real homework assignment, and bring the result to the meeting. A sample of their dictated writing next to their handwritten work is usually the most persuasive thing in the room.
